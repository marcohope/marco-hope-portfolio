Halix Solutions is a custom AI automation studio for social-media marketing agencies. Taking it from nothing to a live studio meant learning the parts of a 0-to-1 that never show up on a résumé line — and I owned the parts I'm strongest at: the design and the front-end, across the marketing site and the systems clients actually use.

The full breakdown lives in [the case study](/work). This is the shorter version of what building it taught me.

## The creative stays human. The busywork doesn't.

The whole studio runs on one boundary: AI shouldn't touch the work an agency's clients can see. Content, graphics, strategy, brand voice — that stays human. Everything behind it — lead discovery, scoring, outbound, follow-ups, reporting — gets a system.

That line isn't just positioning; it's a design constraint. Every automation has to earn its keep by staying firmly on the back-office side of it. Draw it wrong and you've replaced the thing an agency actually gets paid for. Draw it right and you've handed them their evenings back.

## Research before a single screen

Nothing gets built off a guess. Every engagement starts with client meetings that map an agency's real workflow and surface exactly which automations their business needs. Only then does design start.

That order matters more than it sounds. It's the difference between building the automation someone asked for and the one they actually needed — and the research is where that gap closes, long before any interface exists.

## A demo you can see, then a prototype you can click

The research becomes a demo that shows the client what the automation will look like — UI and all — so an abstract "we'll automate your outreach" turns into something concrete they can react to. From there I iterate it into a working prototype deployed on Vercel: not a mockup, the real thing behind a live URL.

That loop is where the front-end work concentrates. I research best practices for each surface and fold in the client's direction, so the software ends up as the client intended rather than as the default the tools nudge you toward. Iterating in the open, against a real URL, keeps the feedback honest.

## One design language, two surfaces

The work lives in two places that pull in opposite directions: a marketing site that has to *sell*, and the client-facing systems that have to *work*. The discipline is refusing to let them drift into two products — one token set, one component vocabulary, so a button on the landing page and a button inside a client's system read as the same hand.

Underneath, it ships to the client's own subdomain on a deliberately modern, well-resourced stack — Next.js and React up front, PostgreSQL and Python behind it — wired to whatever the workflow actually touches, essentially any service with an API.

## What it taught me

Building at 0-to-1 forces a kind of honesty. There's no brief to hide behind when a call comes out wrong, and the unglamorous decisions still have to get made. You learn to make pragmatic trade-offs under real constraints, which corners are load-bearing and which you can leave rough for now, and that shipping is a series of those judgments — made quickly, owned honestly.
